What exactly do you study again?

Every day, people rely on communication to create a shared understanding of the world around them with others (often in order to coordinate with each other in interesting ways). Think: refining your understanding of a journal article by talking about it with your colleagues, or discussing social stressors with your friend group.

My research leverages tools from communication theory, information theory, and natural language processing to understand how people co-create meaning together in dialogs like these.

How People Dynamically Create Common Ground During Interactions
LLMs; Information Theory; Conversation; Pragmatics; Comparative Linguistics; Quantitative Methods

What do you get when you mix information theory with a deep interest in how people create common ground?

Much of this work centers on the idea that when people communicate, everything we say adds a little information. "Information" here is statistical, and it's generated by moving away from ideas and concepts that were said earlier in conversation. If everything a person says adds some information, then the way we stack information up over the course of a conversation is an important part of how we create (and change) common ground, across languages and cultures.

My work in this area makes use of language technologies like Large Language Models, and statistical frameworks like information theory, to better understand what makes a conversation work. And, potentially, guide folks with novel insights into how to have better, more meaningful conversations in a data driven way.


The effects of Hate Speech on Measures of Social Language Use
Information Theory; Social Media Studies; Hate Speech; Group Communication

Research into hate speech tends to focus on one of two things: (1) How hate speech affects the groups and identities targeted by it, and (2) the psychopatholological characteristics of people who use hate speech.

My research in this area address two different (though important) blind spots in this literature:

(1) When "haters" get together with their peers and starting generating hateful content, this has an important observable effect on discourse--information diversity precipitously drops. From a haters' eye view, using hate speech means that the ideas you express will get carried forward in conversation longer and more powerfully.

This becomes a massive problem, because (2) when haters see others "converging" with their ideas, then they're more likely to become more blatantly hateful in subsequent speech.


Differences in LLM and Human Discourse Dynamics
LLMs; Information Theory; Pedagogy

Despite being lauded as open-domain agents, LLMs exhibit strong differences from humans when it comes to how they generate information in discourse when compared to humans. Some of my recent work shows that these differences may be due to LLMs being incapable of replicating human motivations for introducing novelty into ongoing conversations. And work with some very talented undergraduate students seems to show that this also shows up as a strong agreement bias!

In addition to these findings, I work on formal proofs showing that tuning LLM behavior to accomplish typical NLP tasks--like intent classification and topic modeling--might actually be largely intractable. This line of work focuses on defining the information theoretic requirements of such tasks, and the degree to which we can be confident that unstructured LLM responses can meet those requirements.


Processes of information generation in non-neurotypical communication
Information theory; Conversation; Neurodiversity

If communication unfolds as people adding new ifnormation to conversation, how do different neurological configurations, ranging from ASD to TBI, show up in information generation processes?

While this is a relatively nascent interest, preliminary forays into this area show that differences in neurology do indeed impact how we engage in information generation processes together.

Development of adult-like information generation behaviors
Information theory; Conversation; child development

We've talked a deal above about how adults generate and wield information. But where does that com from? Do children learn how to wield and generate information during conversation as they develop? Or is the process and shape of how humans generate information together innate (and the only thing kids learn is the vocabulary they need to do it better)? Or is it something entirely different? An exciting new branch of my research is looking at exactly this question.
